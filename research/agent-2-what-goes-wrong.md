## Findings in detail

**1. Mandatory arbitration & class-action waivers**
- Evidence + quoted figure: CFPB's 2015 study found "tens of millions of consumers are covered by arbitration clauses in the consumer finance markets studied," with card issuers holding arbitration clauses representing over half of all credit card debt (~80 million consumers). "Over three quarters acknowledged they did not know whether their credit card agreement contained an arbitration clause," and "fewer than 7 percent" of those with such clauses understood they could not sue in court. Separately, "at least 160 million class members were eligible for relief over the five-year period studied," with settlements totaling $2.7 billion — relief that arbitration clauses block when invoked. A Center for Justice & Democracy fact sheet adds that "56.2% of private-sector nonunion employees" (over 60 million workers) are subject to forced arbitration, "41.1% of those workers also waived their right to participate in class actions" (~24.7 million workers), and that consumers "win only 9% of arbitration disputes" while companies win relief 93% of the time when counterclaiming, leaving consumers owing an average of $7,725.
- Who it hits: Credit card and consumer-finance customers; private-sector nonunion employees; e-commerce consumers (the same fact sheet cites over 60% of U.S. retail e-commerce sales falling under broad consumer arbitration agreements).
- Why hard to spot: The clause sits in a "Dispute Resolution" or "Governing Law" section late in the document, phrased procedurally (incorporating AAA/JAMS rules by reference) rather than in plain terms like "you cannot sue us or join a class action" — which is exactly why CFPB found three-quarters of consumers didn't know their own contract had one.
- Source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-study-finds-that-arbitration-agreements-limit-relief-for-consumers/ ; https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers

**2. Auto-renewal / negative-option billing**
- Evidence + quoted figure: The FTC reported negative-option/subscription complaints rising to "nearly 70 consumer complaints each day" in 2024, up from "42 per day" in 2021 — roughly a 67% increase in three years — and said it receives "thousands of complaints about negative option and recurring subscription practices every year." This complaint trend is what drove the FTC's 2024 "click-to-cancel" amendments to the Negative Option Rule, requiring cancellation to be "as easy to use as the mechanism used to enroll." State enforcement has produced concrete penalties: HelloFresh "paid $7.5 million to settle a lawsuit brought by two California district attorney's offices" over enrollment and cancellation issues, and a 33-state coalition secured a "$4.8 million settlement with TFG Holding, Inc." over deceptive billing and automatic enrollment.
- Who it hits: Subscription consumers broadly — streaming, gyms, meal kits, SaaS, and any ToS with recurring billing.
- Why hard to spot: The renewal trigger and cancellation deadline (e.g., "must cancel 30 days before renewal") is usually one date-math sentence buried in a "Billing" or "Term" section, and the actual cancellation mechanism is often deferred to an external portal or phone process not described in the contract text itself.
- Source: https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2024/10/22/ftc-issues-final-click-to-cancel-rule-to-make-it-easier-for-consumers-to-cancel-enrollment-in-negative-option-programs/ ; https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/02/ftc-and-state-ags-continue-to-scrutinize-subscription-practices

**3. Late fees / penalty and fee-escalator clauses**
- Evidence + quoted figure: The CFPB found credit card late fees "cost American families more than $14 billion a year," hitting "more than 45 million people," with the typical fee running "from $32 to $8" under the (subsequently contested) rule — an average savings of "more than $10 billion in late fees annually," about $220/year per affected person. An earlier CFPB report found late fees rose from $23 (end of 2010) to $32 (2022), a 28% year-over-year jump in total fees charged between 2021 and 2022 ($14.5B), and that subprime accounts — only 12% of accounts in 2019 — incurred 42% of late-fee volume, showing the burden concentrates on already-strained borrowers.
- Who it hits: Consumer credit card holders directly; the same fee-escalator mechanics (fixed penalty + compounding/CPI-linked increases) recur in leases (late rent fees) and vendor/SaaS contracts (annual price-increase clauses).
- Why hard to spot: The dollar figure is often cross-referenced to a separate "Schedule of Fees" rather than stated in the clause itself, and escalator language ("fees may increase up to X% annually") requires the reader to do multi-year compounding math to see the real cost — something a plain first read won't surface.
- Source: https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-bans-excessive-credit-card-late-fees-lowers-typical-fee-from-32-to-8/

**4. Non-compete / restrictive covenants**
- Evidence + quoted figure: The FTC's 2024 non-compete rule was estimated to affect "approximately 18% of US workers," described elsewhere as "approximately one in five American workers—or approximately 30 million workers." This was significant enough that FTC called it "one of the most sweeping labor market regulations the agency ever issued." (Note: a federal court blocked enforcement of this rule in August 2024, and the FTC moved to dismiss its appeal in September 2025, so the clause type remains largely unregulated at the federal level even though the underlying prevalence data stands.)
- Who it hits: Employment agreements — not limited to executives; FTC's rulemaking record targeted rank-and-file workers as well as vendor/service contractors (e.g., cited enforcement targets included companies with 1,800 to 18,000+ employees).
- Why hard to spot: Rarely labeled "non-compete" in the section heading — usually folded into "Restrictive Covenants," "Confidentiality," or "Post-Termination Obligations," with the actual geographic and time scope defined in a cross-referenced schedule/exhibit rather than in the body clause.
- Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/04/ftc-announces-rule-banning-noncompetes (via search extraction) ; https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/ftc-noncompetes-employers-2026/

**5. Late/nonpayment and missing payment-terms protections (freelancer contracts)**
- Evidence + quoted figure: A Freelancers Union survey (with Authors Guild, Graphic Artist Guild, and other creator organizations) of NY-based freelancers found "62% of freelance workers based in New York had lost wages at least once in their career from their employer's refusal to pay them." "53% reported losing as much as $10,000 to nonpayment from completed work." "91% of survey respondents reported experiencing late or overdue wages at least once in their career with 54% reporting experiencing delays of 3 months or longer," and "39% of respondents said non or overdue payment affected their ability to pay bills or rent." This data underpinned New York City's Freelance Isn't Free Act, which — per Freelancers Union's own reporting — helped freelancers collect over $2 billion in owed payments since the law passed (this last figure could not be independently verified against the NYC DCA's own five-year report, which returned an access error on direct fetch; it is reported here as sourced only to the Freelancers Union blog, with that caveat).
- Who it hits: Freelance/independent-contractor agreements across creative, media, and professional-services work — populations without standard employer payroll protections.
- Why hard to spot: The harm here is frequently an absence rather than a hidden trap — contracts often lack any specific due date, late-payment penalty, or kill fee for cancelled work, and a plain-English reader has no way to flag a clause that isn't there.
- Source: https://blog.freelancersunion.org/2022/05/12/over-60-of-ny-freelancers-report-not-being-paid-for-work-performed/

**6. Limitation of liability & indemnification**
- Evidence + quoted figure: A World Commerce & Contracting (WorldCC/IACCM) "Most Negotiated Terms" report ranks Limitation of Liability as the #1 most negotiated contract term (score 3.1 in the report's methodology), ahead of Price/Charge changes (2.9) and tied near Indemnification (2.9), which ranked #3, both ahead of Termination (2.7) and Payment terms (2.6). Direct fetch of the WorldCC PDF was blocked (403); this ranking is drawn from the search engine's extraction of that document. A secondary source, Icertis, independently confirms the underlying claim: "According to a World Commerce & Contracting report, [limitation of liability] is one of the most commonly negotiated terms between two parties."
- Who it hits: B2B/commercial contracts — vendor, SaaS, consulting, and freelance service agreements where one party tries to cap its total financial exposure or shift risk to the other.
- Why hard to spot: Caps are usually defined by cross-reference (e.g., "fees paid in the preceding 12 months") rather than a plain dollar figure, and whether an indemnification obligation is mutual or one-sided — and whether carve-outs exist for gross negligence or IP infringement — requires comparison to a market baseline a non-lawyer doesn't have.
- Source: https://www.worldcc.com/Portals/IACCM/Resources/9934_0_Most%20Negotiated%20Terms%202020.pdf (fetch blocked; cited via search extraction) ; https://www.icertis.com/contracting-basics/limitation-of-liability-clause/ (corroborating, fetched directly)
- Caveat on evidence type: this measures how often sophisticated, counsel-represented parties choose to negotiate the term pre-signing — a different signal from consumer complaint/harm volume. It is included because it is the clearest available evidence for B2B contract friction, but the ranking position reflects negotiation frequency, not documented post-signing harm to non-lawyers.

**7. Security deposit / repair-liability clauses (residential leases)**
- Evidence + quoted figure: Rental Protection Agency data, reported via American Apartment Owners Association, states that "security deposit disputes ranked number one" on its list of most common apartment/tenant complaints nationwide, compiled from "thousands of tenant-filed complaints across all 50 states." The dispute driver most cited is ambiguity between deductible "damage" and non-deductible "normal wear and tear."
- Who it hits: Residential tenants broadly.
- Why hard to spot: Leases rarely define "normal wear and tear" with any specificity, leaving landlords wide discretion on itemized deductions, and repair-liability language often shifts responsibility for pre-existing conditions onto the tenant through boilerplate "accepts premises as-is" phrasing that reads as routine.
- Source: https://american-apartment-owners-association.org/property-management/latest-news/10-most-common-apartment-complaints/

## How confident the ranking is

- **Evidence-driven, high confidence**: Ranks 1–4 (arbitration, auto-renewal, late fees, non-compete) rest on federal regulatory bodies (CFPB, FTC) publishing their own complaint volumes, study findings, or rulemaking impact estimates — the strongest tier of evidence available for this task.
- **Evidence-driven, moderate confidence**: Rank 5 (freelancer nonpayment) rests on a real, large-sample survey but is geographically limited (NY-based respondents) and advocacy-sponsored (Freelancers Union and allied guilds), not a neutral government dataset — treat the percentages as directionally strong but not nationally representative.
- **Judgment call, thin evidence**: Ranks 6–7 (limitation of liability/indemnification, security deposit) are placed lower not because the harm is smaller but because the best available evidence measures a different thing than consumer harm — WorldCC data measures negotiation frequency among represented parties, and the tenant-complaint ranking comes from an industry blog citing an advocacy organization's compilation rather than a government or peer-reviewed count. Their relative order (6 above 7) is a judgment call based on WorldCC's data being closer to a structured methodology (numeric negotiation-frequency scores across many organizations) versus the tenant source's single unranked "#1" claim with no underlying counts. A researcher with more search budget should verify both against CFPB or HUD tenant-complaint data and a a more recent/accessible WorldCC report before treating this order as final.
- A general note on context: CFPB's overall consumer-complaint database is dominated by credit-reporting accuracy issues (roughly 79% of 2023 complaint volume) and debt collection (~7%), which are not primarily contract-clause disputes in the sense this assignment is investigating (they're about data accuracy and collection conduct, not a clause the person signed). This suggests that clause-specific harm, as measured here, is a real but comparatively narrow slice of the total consumer-complaint landscape — most complaint volume is about something other than "what a contract clause allowed."

## What I could not find

- **IP assignment / work-for-hire overreach**: No dedicated search was run for this within the 12-search budget (it was deprioritized in favor of clause types with clearer regulatory paper trails), so no sourced frequency/harm evidence was found. This is a genuine gap, not a negative finding — it may well be a real, common Redline use case, but I have no citation to support ranking it.
- **Personal guarantees**: Search returned only general small-business loan default-rate statistics (4–12% annual default rates; SBA loans under $150K defaulting 2–3x more than larger loans), with no data connecting the personal-guarantee clause itself to complaint volume or documented harm distinct from ordinary loan default. Dropped as unsupported per the guardrail rather than treated as a finding.
- **Exclusivity clauses, unilateral-modification-of-terms clauses (outside auto-renewal), and data/content licensing in ToS**: Not searched within budget; no evidence gathered either way.
- **NYC DCA's own five-year Freelance Isn't Free Act report**: Direct fetch was blocked (403), so the $2.1B recovered figure and detailed violation-type breakdown could not be independently verified against the primary government report — it is reported above only as sourced to the Freelancers Union blog's characterization, with that caveat flagged.
- **A precise ranking method that combines complaint volume across very different data sources** (CFPB counts vs. FTC rulemaking estimates vs. survey percentages vs. B2B negotiation-frequency scores) does not exist in the literature I found — the "ranked table" above is my synthesis across incompatible metrics, not a single study's ranking, and should be read that way.
