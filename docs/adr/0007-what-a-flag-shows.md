# 7. What a flag shows, and where its facts come from

A flag shows what the clause does to the reader, and any exit or opt-out the
document itself grants, alongside its severity and confidence. It does not
recommend whether to sign.

Consequences are stated in two tiers. The document-grounded claim leads and
carries its source sentence. Anything from outside the document sits beneath it,
marked as external and carrying its own citation.

## Why

"You cannot join a class action against them" is in the document. "Consumers win
only 9% of arbitrations" is CFPB data. The second is what makes the first land,
but presenting them in one voice would breach the rule that we state only what the
document says. Separating them keeps the principle that actually matters — the
reader can check everything they are told — without discarding the material that
makes a flag mean anything.

A sign/don't-sign verdict was considered and rejected. It is not in the document,
and it is the shape of claim the FTC fined DoNotPay $193,000 for making.

## Consequences

- Exit instructions cover only deadlines and opt-outs the document states.
  Statutory rights, such as state cancellation laws, go unmentioned even where the
  reader holds them (ADR 0005).
- External context requires its own sourced fact base, and its accuracy is ours.
- A flag now carries four things: consequence, exit, severity, confidence. How a
  lay reader is meant to combine them is still open (ADR 0003).
